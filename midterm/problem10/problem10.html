Problem 10

I have a lot of opinions on this since I used to do databases for my day job. I'll try to be concise.

Schema

The most significant difference is that the relational database paradigm is focused on the idea of a schema. Every RDB has a schema defined in terms of tables and keys and whatnot.

There are schemaful NoSQL databases, which use some kind of alternate notion of a schema than just tables and columns and keys. However, there are also schema-less NoSQL databases (like of course Mongo) which don't have a schema at all.

Language

The standard language for a relational database is right proper SQL, like Codd intended.

NoSQL databases have their own methods. Mongo doesn't really have a language at all, beyond basic JSON notation.

Something like BigTable or Neo4j, meanwhile, have their own "*QL" languages, like Neo4j's own GQL (Graph Query Language).

ACID Compliance

ACID compliance is a requirement for a relational DB according to the SQL standard.

ACID compliance is a not assured for all NoSQL database engines. Some do, some don't.

This is why (in my professional opinion) you're almost always going to end up back at a SQL database for your core operations. I can't deny that there are great use cases for NoSQL systems, but your core data wants ACID compliance, so you can easily maintain atomic backups and so that your systems don't burn down when a query fails.

"""Scale"""

Another difference people will tell you is the scaling issues between SQL and NoSQL databases. For example, a NoSQL database like the ddb under Apache Spark will 'scale' infinitely sideways by just adding more machines.

I, however, hasten to point out the following:

  1. Does your organization REALLY need infinite scaling? Chances are you can reasonably estimate both your typical usage and your best-case growth and scale enough SQL cores to handle it. Plus, after a certain size, you just can't respond rapidly enough running on anything other than redis, so you're really bottlenecking at your db-to-redis pipeline, not at the db engine itself.
  2. Almost all "scaling" issues are engine optimization issues anyway. Hire a good DB specialist and stop using amateurish queries. Build high-speed views and normalize your data and you'll be amazed how much faster it all gets.
